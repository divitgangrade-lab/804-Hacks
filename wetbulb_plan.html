The idea in plain English

1 · Computers get hot

Data centers sweat

A data center is a warehouse full of computers that run hot. Many cool off with cooling towers, which work like sweating: they evaporate water to carry the heat away.

2 · Sticky days are bad

Humid heat wastes water

Sweating barely works on a hot, sticky day, and cooling towers are the same: they burn through more water. The number that measures this is the wet-bulb temperature. You calculate it from temperature plus humidity, which is exactly what your DHT11 sensor reads.

3 · Power changes hourly

The grid has good and bad hours

Virginia's electricity comes from a mix of coal, gas, nuclear and solar, and the mix shifts every hour. Some hours make more CO₂. Power plants also use water: nuclear makes no smoke but uses a lot of water.

4 · The trick

Run the job at the right time

Lots of AI work can wait a few hours, like training a model or making backups. Wetbulb looks at the next 24 hours and picks the window with the least carbon and the least water.

The surprise that makes it interesting

Carbon and water might want different hours. Cool nights save cooling water, but if the night grid runs mostly on nuclear, it uses a lot of power-plant water. A plain carbon tracker can't see that. The first thing your Kaggle script checks is which story the data actually tells:

If they conflict

"The cleanest hour is the thirstiest hour." The slider on the dashboard becomes the star: you pick how much you care about carbon versus water.

If they agree

"The night shift wins twice." The story is simpler. One shift saves both, and nobody schedules for water today.

Your 15-second pitch

"Virginia has more data centers than anywhere on Earth, and they have two footprints, carbon and water. Everyone tracks the first. Wetbulb tracks both, live, and tells a data center when to run its AI jobs. Watch our rack live through a real July day in Ashburn."

How the pieces fit

The Arduino doesn't decide anything. It sends readings to the browser, and the browser sends back the two LCD lines and the fan speed. That keeps the Arduino code tiny and keeps all the logic in one place.

What the script does

  1. Loads the PJM fuel mix and computes carbon (g CO₂/kWh) and power-plant water (L/kWh) for every hour.
  2. Pulls Ashburn temperature, humidity and wet-bulb for the same hours, falling back to the Philadelphia climatology if needed.
  3. Computes data center cooling water from the wet-bulb temperature (model below).
  4. Story check: month × hour heatmaps for both footprints, the correlation between them, and the number of days where the cleanest window ≠ the thirstiest window. It prints a clear verdict: CONFLICT or ALIGNED.
  5. Backtest: a 4-hour, 500 kW job submitted at 9 AM with a 24-hour deadline, run every day. Compares 4 policies: run now, carbon-only, water-only, and Wetbulb (blended).
  6. Trade-off curve: sweeps the slider from all-carbon to all-water and plots CO₂ saved against water saved.
  7. Virginia scale-up: if a given share of NoVA's data center load were flexible and shifted, how many tons of CO₂ and Olympic pools of water would that save each year? Assumptions are printed.
  8. Exports wetbulb_profile.json (for the dashboard), results.json (for the slides), replay_days.json (3 real Ashburn days for the replay demo) and 5 PNG charts.

What the DHT11 measures

The sensor reads the air right around it: temperature (°C) and relative humidity (%), about once a second. It's accurate to roughly ±2 °C and ±5% RH. In the story, it stands for the outdoor air going into a data center's cooling tower in Ashburn. It doesn't measure how hot the servers are. Cooling-tower water depends on the outside air, not on how hot the chips run.

The dashboard turns each reading into a wet-bulb temperature, then into cooling water per kWh. The sensor stands in for the current hour. The other 23 hours come from the forecast.

Questions judges will ask

Is your water model real?The power-plant water figures come from NREL. The cooling curve is a simplified model tuned to the published US average, and we label it that way.
Average or marginal emissions?Average, because it's public and hourly. Marginal is the better signal and the top item on our next-steps slide.
